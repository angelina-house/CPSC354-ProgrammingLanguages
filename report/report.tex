\subsection*{Homework}
Discuss the MU puzzle from \emph{Gödel, Escher, Bach} (Chapter 1; see course PDF pp.~41--42). Does the string \texttt{MU} have a derivation in the MIU formal system starting from \texttt{MI}? Why or why not?

\subsubsection*{The MU (MIU) System Recap}
We begin with the single axiom string \texttt{MI}. The system has four production rules:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item If a string ends in \texttt{I}, append a \texttt{U} at the end.
  \item If the string has the form \texttt{M}x, duplicate everything after \texttt{M}: \texttt{M}x $\to$ \texttt{M}xx.
  \item Replace any occurrence of \texttt{III} by \texttt{U}.
  \item Delete any occurrence of \texttt{UU}.
\end{enumerate}

From \texttt{MI}, we can derive many strings by applying the rules:
\[
\texttt{MI} \xRightarrow{(2)} \texttt{MII} \xRightarrow{(2)} \texttt{MIIII} \xRightarrow{(3)} \texttt{MUI}.
\]
This illustrates how the rules expand and compress substrings, but it remains unclear how to end with exactly \texttt{MU}.

Let $I(s)$ be the number of \texttt{I}'s in a string $s$. Initially, $I(\texttt{MI})=1$, i.e., $1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)$. Examine how each rule affects $I$ modulo $3$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Rule 1 adds a \texttt{U}; $I$ is unchanged.
  \item Rule 2 doubles the count of \texttt{I}'s: $I \mapsto 2I$. Thus if $I \equiv 1\pmod{3}$ then $2I \equiv 2\pmod{3}$.
  \item Rule 3 subtracts three \texttt{I}'s (\texttt{III} $\to$ \texttt{U}); the residue modulo $3$ is unchanged.
  \item Rule 4 deletes \texttt{U}'s only; $I$ is unchanged.
\end{itemize}
Therefore, starting from $I \equiv 1\pmod{3}$, any derivable string keeps $I \equiv 1$ or $I \equiv 2\pmod{3}$, but never $0\pmod{3}$. The target \texttt{MU} has $I(\texttt{MU})=0$ ($0\pmod{3}$), so it is \emph{not} derivable. Hence, the MU puzzle has no solution.

The puzzle shows how stepping outside a formal system and using an invariant (here, the count of \texttt{I}'s modulo 3) can decisively answer a reachability question that seems open-ended from within the rules.

\subsubsection*{Reading Summary \& Question}
\textbf{Summary.} Hofstadter introduces formal systems via the MIU puzzle and contrasts \emph{inside-the-system} rule manipulation with \emph{meta-level} reasoning using invariants. This frames later topics on meaning, proof, and limitations of formal systems.

\textbf{Question.} Are there any situations where the MU puzzle could be true, and how much would we have to change to get to that point?

\subsection*{Homework}
Using the course notes on abstract reduction systems (ARS), determine for each of the seven given ARSs whether it is \emph{terminating}, \emph{confluent}, and whether it \emph{has unique normal forms (UN)}. Then, fill the grid of the eight possible \((\text{Confluent}, \text{Terminating}, \text{Has UN})\) combinations with examples or explain why some combinations are impossible.

\subsubsection*{Quick Definitions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Terminating:} there is no infinite chain \(x_0 \to x_1 \to x_2 \to \cdots\).
  \item \textbf{Confluent:} if \(x \to^* y\) and \(x \to^* z\), then \(y\) and \(z\) are joinable.
  \item \textbf{Has unique normal forms (UN):} every element has a (and in fact the) unique normal form.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Seven ARSs: Diagrams \& Properties}
Below, each ARS is shown as a tiny diagram (compiled with \texttt{tikz-cd}) followed by its properties and a one–line justification.

\paragraph{1) \(A=\varnothing\).}
\[
\text{(empty set, no nodes or arrows)}
\]
\textbf{Props:} Terminating \(\checkmark\), Confluent \(\checkmark\), UN \(\checkmark\). \emph{Reason:} All properties hold vacuously.

\paragraph{2) \(A=\{a\},\ R=\varnothing\).}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
a
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\textbf{Props:} Terminating \(\checkmark\), Confluent \(\checkmark\), UN \(\checkmark\). \emph{Reason:} No arrows; \(a\) is a (unique) normal form.

\paragraph{3) \(A=\{a\},\ R=\{(a,a)\}\).}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
a \arrow[out=45,in=135,loop, ""]
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\textbf{Props:} Terminating \(\times\), Confluent \(\checkmark\), UN \(\times\). \emph{Reason:} Self–loop gives infinite chain; only one object so forks (if any) are trivially joinable; no normal form exists.

\paragraph{4) \(A=\{a,b,c\},\ R=\{(a,b),(a,c)\}\).}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
& a \arrow[dl] \arrow[dr] & \\
b & & c
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\textbf{Props:} Terminating \(\checkmark\), Confluent \(\times\), UN \(\times\). \emph{Reason:} From \(a\) reach distinct NFs \(b\) and \(c\) that are not joinable.

\paragraph{5) \(A=\{a,b\},\ R=\{(a,a),(a,b)\}\).}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
a \arrow[loop left] \arrow[r] & b
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\textbf{Props:} Terminating \(\times\), Confluent \(\checkmark\), UN \(\checkmark\). \emph{Reason:} There exists an infinite chain via the loop on \(a\), but all reductions from \(a\) can join at \(b\), and every element’s normal form is uniquely \(b\).

\paragraph{6) \(A=\{a,b,c\},\ R=\{(a,b),(b,b),(a,c)\}\).}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
& a \arrow[dl] \arrow[dr] & \\
b \arrow[loop left] & & c
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\textbf{Props:} Terminating \(\times\), Confluent \(\times\), UN \(\times\). \emph{Reason:} Loop at \(b\) breaks termination; from \(a\) you can reach \(b\) (non–NF forever) or \(c\) (an NF), so confluence and UN fail.

\paragraph{7) \(A=\{a,b,c\},\ R=\{(a,b),(b,b),(a,c),(c,c)\}\).}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
& a \arrow[dl] \arrow[dr] & \\
b \arrow[loop left] & & c \arrow[loop right]
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\textbf{Props:} Terminating \(\times\), Confluent \(\times\), UN \(\times\). \emph{Reason:} Loops at \(b\) and \(c\); from \(a\) diverge to non–joinable loops; no normal forms.

\subsubsection*{Exercise 5 — Analysis}
\textbf{Rules:}
\[
\texttt{ab}\to\texttt{ba},\quad \texttt{ba}\to\texttt{ab},\quad \texttt{aa}\to\varepsilon.
\]

\paragraph{Sample reductions.}
\[
\texttt{abba}\;\Rightarrow\;\texttt{baba}\;\xRightarrow{\texttt{ba}\to\texttt{ab}}\;\texttt{bbaa}\;\xRightarrow{\texttt{aa}\to\varepsilon}\;\texttt{bb}.
\]
\[
\texttt{bababa}\;\Rightarrow\;\texttt{aaabbb}\;\xRightarrow{\texttt{aa}\to\varepsilon}\;\texttt{abbb}.
\]

\paragraph{Non-termination.}
Swaps are bidirectional, so we can permute letters indefinitely; the ARS is not terminating.

\paragraph{Equivalence classes \& invariant.}
Swaps preserve counts of letters. The rule \texttt{aa}$\to\varepsilon$ preserves \(\#a \pmod 2\).  
Thus the complete invariant is
\[
\big(\#\texttt{b},\;\#\texttt{a}\bmod 2\big).
\]

\paragraph{Normal forms via terminating variant.}
If we orient swaps one way (e.g.\ \texttt{ba}$\to$\texttt{ab}) and keep \texttt{aa}$\to\varepsilon$, then every word terminates in a unique normal form:
\[
\texttt{b}^k \quad(\#a \text{ even}), \qquad \texttt{a}\,\texttt{b}^k \quad(\#a \text{ odd}),
\]
where \(k=\#\texttt{b}\).

\subsubsection*{Exercise 5b — Analysis}
\textbf{Rules (variant):}
\[
\texttt{ab}\to\texttt{ba},\quad \texttt{ba}\to\texttt{ab},\quad \texttt{aa}\to\texttt{a}.
\]

\paragraph{Non-termination.}
Still non-terminating because of two-way swaps.

\paragraph{Equivalence classes \& invariant.}
Swaps preserve \(\#\texttt{b}\). The rule \texttt{aa}$\to\texttt{a}\) collapses any number of \texttt{a}’s to exactly one (and in the equivalence closure we can also expand).  
Thus classes are determined by
\[
\big(\#\texttt{b},\;\mathbf{1}_{\{\#a\ge 1\}}\big),
\]
i.e.\ the number of \texttt{b}\,s and whether there is at least one \texttt{a}.

\paragraph{Normal forms via terminating variant.}
If we orient \texttt{ba}$\to$\texttt{ab} and keep \texttt{aa}$\to\texttt{a}, then the unique normal forms are:
\[
\texttt{b}^k \quad(\#a=0), \qquad \texttt{a}\,\texttt{b}^k \quad(\#a\ge 1),
\]
with \(k=\#\texttt{b}\).

\subsection{Week 4}\label{sec:week4}
\subsection*{Homework}
Using a measure function (from the termination notes), prove termination of (1) Euclid’s algorithm for \(\gcd(a,b)\) and (2) a standard top-down merge sort.

\subsubsection*{HW 4.1 — Euclid’s Algorithm Terminates}
\textbf{Algorithm.}
\begin{verbatim}
while b != 0:
    temp = b
    b = a mod b
    a = temp
return a
\end{verbatim}

\textbf{Assumptions.} \(a,b \in \mathbb{N}\) and the loop guard is first checked with \(b \ge 0\).

\textbf{Measure.} \(\phi(a,b) = b \in \mathbb{N}\).

\textbf{Proof.} One loop step maps \((a,b)\) to \((b,\, a \bmod b)\). If \(b \neq 0\), then \(0 \le a \bmod b < b\), hence
\[
\phi(b,\, a \bmod b) = a \bmod b \;<\; b \;=\; \phi(a,b).
\]
Thus \(\phi\) strictly decreases at each taken iteration and always stays in \(\mathbb{N}\). Since \(>\) on \(\mathbb{N}\) is well-founded, there is no infinite strictly decreasing chain; the loop terminates when \(b=0\).

\subsubsection*{HW 4.2 — Merge Sort Terminates}
\textbf{Code (top-down).}
\begin{verbatim}
function merge_sort(arr, left, right):
    if left >= right: return
    mid = (left + right) // 2   // integer division
    merge_sort(arr, left, mid)
    merge_sort(arr, mid+1, right)
    merge(arr, left, mid, right)
\end{verbatim}

\textbf{Measure.} For a call on interval \([left,right]\), let
\[
\phi(left,right) \;=\; right - left + 1 \in \mathbb{N}.
\]

\textbf{Proof.} If \(left \ge right\), the function returns immediately. Otherwise \(left < right\) and with integer division \(left \le mid < right\). The recursive calls are on \([left,\,mid]\) and \([mid{+}1,\,right]\) with measures
\[
\phi(left,mid) = mid - left + 1 \;<\; right - left + 1 = \phi(left,right),
\]
\[
\phi(mid{+}1,right) = right - mid \;<\; right - left + 1 = \phi(left,right).
\]
Hence each recursive call strictly decreases \(\phi\). Because \(\phi\) ranges over \(\mathbb{N}\) and decreases along recursion, there is no infinite descent; all recursion bottoms out and the algorithm terminates. (No measure is needed for \texttt{merge}, which is non-recursive.)

\subsection{Week 5}\label{sec:week5}
\smallskip
\subsection*{Homework}
Evaluate
\[
(\lambda f.\,\lambda x.\, f(f(x)))\;(\lambda f.\,\lambda x.\, f(f(f\,x))).
\]

\subsubsection*{Solution}
Let \(A \equiv \lambda f.\lambda x.\, f(f(x))\) and \(B \equiv \lambda f.\lambda x.\, f(f(f\,x))\).

\paragraph{Step 1.} Apply \(A\) to \(B\):
\[
A\,B \;\to_\beta\; \lambda x.\, B\,(B\,x).
\]

\paragraph{Step 2.} \(\alpha\)-rename inside \(B\) as \(B \equiv \lambda f.\lambda y.\, f(f(f\,y))\) and compute:
\[
B\,x \;=\; (\lambda f.\lambda y.\, f(f(f\,y)))\,x \;\to_\beta\; \lambda y.\, x(x(x\,y)).
\]
Write \(G \equiv \lambda y.\, x(x(x\,y))\). Then
\[
\lambda x.\, B\,(B\,x) \;=\; \lambda x.\, B\,G \;\to_\beta\; \lambda x.\, \lambda y.\, G\big(G(G\,y)\big).
\]

\paragraph{Step 3.} Unroll \(G\):
\[
G\,y = x^{3}(y),\qquad
G(G\,y) = x^{6}(y),\qquad
G\big(G(G\,y)\big) = x^{9}(y).
\]

\subsubsection*{Answer}
\[
\boxed{\lambda x.\,\lambda y.\, x(x(x(x(x(x(x(x(x\,y))))))))}
\;=\;
\lambda x.\lambda y.\, x^{9}(y).
\]
(Interpreting the terms as Church numerals, this is \(3^{2}=9\).)

\paragraph{Remark.}
The left term applies a function twice, the right term thrice; composing them yields ninefold application.

Compute \texttt{fact 3} using the computation rules for \texttt{fix}, \texttt{let}, and \texttt{let rec}, and label each step with the rule used.

\subsubsection*{Solution}
We use the standard sugars/rules:
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\texttt{fix } F \;\to\; (F\,(\texttt{fix } F)) \qquad
&&\texttt{let } x = e_1 \text{ in } e_2 \;\to\; (\lambda x.\,e_2)\,e_1 \\
&\texttt{let rec } f = e_1 \text{ in } e_2 \;\to\; \texttt{let } f = (\texttt{fix }(\lambda f.\,e_1)) \text{ in } e_2
\end{aligned}
\]
and basic computation rules for conditionals and arithmetic:
\[
0=0 \to \texttt{True},\quad n{>}0 \Rightarrow (n=0) \to \texttt{False},\quad
\texttt{if True then }A\texttt{ else }B \to A,\quad
\texttt{if False then }A\texttt{ else }B \to B.
\]
For brevity, write
\[
F \;\stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\; \lambda f.\,\lambda n.\; \texttt{if }(n=0)\texttt{ then }1\texttt{ else }n * f(n-1).

\subsubsection*{Natural-language proof for Level 8 (\(2+2=4\))}
We use Peano naturals where \(\mathrm{succ}(n)\) is the successor, and
\[
0,\quad 1=\mathrm{succ}(0),\quad 2=\mathrm{succ}(1),\quad
3=\mathrm{succ}(2),\quad 4=\mathrm{succ}(3).
\]
Addition is defined by \(a+0=a\) and \(a+\mathrm{succ}(b)=\mathrm{succ}(a+b)\).
Then:
\[
\begin{aligned}
2+2
&= 2+\mathrm{succ}(1) &&\text{(since \(2=\mathrm{succ}(1)\))}\\
&= \mathrm{succ}(2+1) &&\text{(def.\ of addition)}\\
&= \mathrm{succ}\big(2+\mathrm{succ}(0)\big) &&\text{(since \(1=\mathrm{succ}(0)\))}\\
&= \mathrm{succ}\big(\mathrm{succ}(2+0)\big) &&\text{(def.\ of addition)}\\
&= \mathrm{succ}\big(\mathrm{succ}(2)\big) &&\text{(since \(2+0=2\))}\\
&= \mathrm{succ}(3) = 4. &&\qedhere
\end{aligned}
\]

\subsubsection*{Level 5 — Statement}
\begin{theorem}[Right-commutativity of addition]
For all $a,b,c\in\mathbb N$, \; $a+b+c = a+c+b$.
\end{theorem}

\subsubsection*{Lean solution (without induction)}
\begin{lstlisting}
-- Using only associativity and commutativity of +
theorem add_right_comm (a b c : ℕ) : a + b + c = a + c + b := by
  -- a + b + c = a + (b + c)
  rw [add_assoc]
  -- a + (b + c) = a + (c + b)
  rw [add_comm b c]
  -- a + (c + b) = a + c + b
  rw [add_assoc]
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection*{Pen-and-paper proof (corresponding to the non-inductive Lean script)}
We use associativity and commutativity of $+$ on $\mathbb N$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
a+b+c &= a+(b+c) &&\text{(associativity)}\\
      &= a+(c+b) &&\text{(commutativity)}\\
      &= a+c+b   &&\text{(associativity).}
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus $a+b+c=a+c+b$.

\subsubsection*{Lean solution (with induction on \(b\))}
\begin{lstlisting}
theorem add_right_comm (a b c : ℕ) : a + b + c = a + c + b := by
  induction b with
  | zero =>
      -- goal: a + 0 + c = a + c + 0
      rw [add_zero, add_zero]
      rfl
  | succ d ih =>
      -- goal: a + succ d + c = a + c + succ d
      -- expose the IH by rewriting both sides
      rw [add_succ]        -- a + succ d + c = succ (a + d) + c
      rw [succ_add]        -- succ (a + d) + c = succ ((a + d) + c)
      rw [ih]              -- succ ((a + c) + d)
      rw [add_succ]        -- a + c + succ d = succ ((a + c) + d)
      rfl
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection*{Pen-and-paper proof (corresponding to the inductive Lean script)}
\textbf{Claim.} For all $a,c\in\mathbb N$ and all $b\in\mathbb N$, we have $a+b+c=a+c+b$.

\emph{Base case $b=0$.} Using $x+0=x$,
\[
a+0+c = a+c = a+c+0.
\]

\emph{Inductive step.} Suppose $a+d+c=a+c+d$ for some $d$ (induction hypothesis). Using the successor laws
\[
x+(y+1)=(x+y)+1 \quad\text{and}\quad (x+1)+y=(x+y)+1,
\]
we compute
\[
\begin{aligned}
a+(d+1)+c
&= (a+d+1)+c
 = (a+d+c)+1
\\&= (a+c+d)+1 \qquad\text{(by IH)}
\\&= (a+c)+(d+1)
= a+c+(d+1).
\end{aligned}
\]
Hence the property holds for $d+1$, and by induction it holds for all $b\in\mathbb N$. \qed

So I expected

Running the interpreter confirmed these expectations.

\paragraph{Composed Church numerals.}
From Homework 5 we know that
\[

is the composition of ``apply twice'' with ``apply three times.'' Writing

\mathbf{2} \;\equiv\; \lambda f.\lambda x.\, f(f(x)), \qquad
\mathbf{3} \;\equiv\; \lambda f.\lambda x.\, f(f(f(x))),
\]
the term \(\mathbf{2}\,\mathbf{3}\) should behave like the Church numeral for \(3^2=9\):

\mathbf{2}\,\mathbf{3}\;\equiv\;\lambda x.\,\mathbf{3}(\mathbf{3}\,x)

\subsubsection*{Item 3 --- How Capture-Avoiding Substitution Works}
Conceptually, substitution \([N/x]M\) replaces all free occurrences of \(x\) in \(M\) by \(N\), while avoiding variable capture.

In the Python interpreter this is implemented structurally on the abstract syntax tree:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Variables are represented by a small \texttt{Var} node with a name.
  \item Lambda abstractions are nodes \texttt{Abs(name, body)}.
  \item Applications are nodes \texttt{App(func, arg)}.
\end{itemize}
Substitution is a recursive function \texttt{substitute(term, name, argument)}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item For a variable node \texttt{x}:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item If \texttt{x.name == name}, return the argument term.
      \item Otherwise return \texttt{x} unchanged.
    \end{itemize}
  \item For an application \texttt{App(M,N)}, recursively substitute into both \texttt{M} and \texttt{N}.
  \item For an abstraction \texttt{Abs(x,M)}:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item If \texttt{x == name}, the bound parameter shadows the name, so do nothing under this binder and return \texttt{Abs(x,M)}.
      \item Otherwise, compute the free variables of the argument. If \texttt{x} appears free in the argument, generate a fresh name \texttt{y} (e.g.\ \texttt{Var1}, \texttt{Var2}, \dots), rename the binder and its bound occurrences in \texttt{M} to \texttt{y}, and then continue the substitution in the renamed body.
      \item If there is no clash, recursively substitute into the body.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
So capture-avoidance is enforced by renaming binders just before they might capture a free variable in the argument. This matches the mathematical picture from lecture: we are always free to \(\alpha\)-rename bound variables so that the \(\beta\)-reduction step \( (\lambda x.M)\,N \to M[N/x]\) does not change the meaning of the program.

  \item Church arithmetic (successor, addition, multiplication) behaves as specified on small numerals.

However, not \emph{all} lambda-terms reduce to normal form. For self-application terms like
\[
\Omega \;\equiv\; (\lambda x.\, x\,x)\,(\lambda x.\, x\,x)
\]
the interpreter keeps reducing forever: each step recreates the same redex. This demonstrates the general fact that the untyped lambda calculus is Turing complete and has non-terminating programs.

\subsubsection*{Item 5 --- A Minimal Non-Normalizing Example}
A classic minimal working example (MWE) of a lambda-term without normal form is
\[
\Omega \;\equiv\; (\lambda x.\, x\,x)\,(\lambda x.\, x\,x).
\]
A single \(\lambda\)-abstraction, such as \(\lambda x.x\), is already in normal form. Any application \(M\,N\) with smaller subterms \(M,N\) either reduces to a smaller term or normalizes quickly. In contrast, \(\Omega\) reduces to itself:
\[
(\lambda x.\, x\,x)\,(\lambda x.\, x\,x) \;\to_\beta\;
(\lambda x.\, x\,x)\,(\lambda x.\, x\,x),
\]
so the reduction sequence never terminates. My expectation (confirmed by the interpreter) is that evaluating \(\Omega\) loops forever.
